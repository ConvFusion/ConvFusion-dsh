# Skill: Research Direction Review

## Purpose

Judge whether a stated direction can actually be advanced into a contribution — and when it cannot, produce an **executable route back to a workable direction**: what was wrong, where to look instead, and a concrete literature search that will find it.

This is the review to run when the input is a *direction*, not a result. A blocked direction is a normal outcome of honest searching, and the useful output is a new searchable space plus the criteria for choosing inside it — not encouragement, and not a topic handed down from above.

## When to Use

Use this skill when:

- A topic or direction exists but its viability is unverified, and work has not yet been committed to plans and experiments.
- A direction has stalled: results do not discriminate, the gap turned out to be occupied, or the question cannot be answered with the means available.
- A researcher cannot find a topic worth seeking guidance on, and needs to know how to search differently rather than search more.
- A mentor receives a direction that cannot be advanced and must redirect the search instead of critiquing results that do not exist yet.

Use the research-quality review when a body of work exists; use this skill when what exists is a direction.

## Research Method

1. **Restate the direction as a question that could be answered.** Convert the topic into the research question it implies, and say what would count as an answer. A direction that cannot be restated as an answerable question has already produced its first finding.
2. **Separate the four independent reasons a direction fails.** Name which hold, and how you know:
   - **the problem** — it is not a problem: no one needs the answer, or nothing follows from it;
   - **the gap** — it is occupied: the closest work already answers it (or answers most of it);
   - **the means** — it cannot be answered with the available data, compute, instruments, access, ethics approval or time;
   - **the visibility** — even if answered, no one could tell: the difference is not observable, or it would not change what anyone does.
   These have different remedies. Treating a means problem as a topic problem is the most common misdiagnosis.
3. **Audit the search that produced the direction before trusting its gap.** Record what was actually searched — databases, query strings, date window, screening rules, and where the search stopped. Then ask: were the closest competing works found, or only works sharing keywords; is the gap a real absence or an area unfamiliar to the searcher; was the search abandoned at the first plausible framing. **Unknown coverage makes the gap unverified, not open.**
4. **Test whether the direction lives in a live area.** Does the community measure, argue about and cite this kind of result? Where would it be published, and who would use it? If no venue or readership can be named with evidence, the contribution is unverified.
5. **Apply the discrimination tests, each pass/fail with its reason.** Would the answer change a decision anyone makes? Could the result come out the other way and still be informative? Is it distinguishable from applying a known method to another dataset? Is there a simpler explanation or an existing equivalent? Is it a restatement of something already established under different words?
6. **If the direction holds, say the minimum it needs to become a research plan** — the gap statement, the questions, and the first experiment that could discriminate — and stop. Do not manufacture problems to justify a longer review.
7. **If the direction cannot be advanced, produce the recovery plan. This is the main deliverable, and it must be executable:**
   - **What was wrong**, stated as a finding about the direction, never as a judgement about the researcher.
   - **Where to look next** — choose 2–4 concrete pivots and give the reason each could work: an adjacent problem that shares the method; inverting the assumption that failed; changing the unit (task, dataset, setting, modality, population, scale); chasing the contradiction or anomaly found while searching; moving one level down (mechanism) or up (problem framing); or starting from what practitioners actually do instead.
   - **A re-search plan with real queries** — the databases and venues, 3–8 full query strings per pivot with synonyms and field-specific terms, a date window, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and a **stopping rule** (what evidence indicates the area is crowded or still open).
   - **Discriminating screening rules** — what in a title is enough to include or reject without reading the full text, and what marks the closest competing work once it appears.
   - **A decision gate** — after the re-search, 2–3 candidate directions survive, with the criteria and the time budget that will select one.
8. **Never leave a verdict without a next action.** Every "cannot be advanced" finding is paired with something that produces a new direction; every direction proposed is labelled a **candidate that still needs its own search**, never as a validated topic.
9. **Do not make the choice for the researcher.** Provide candidates, the evidence for each, and the tests that would settle selection; the commitment is theirs.

## Reasoning Guidance

- **"Cannot be advanced" is an outcome, not a failure.** Say it early, plainly and with the reason. A blocked direction found in a week is cheap; the same direction found after a semester is not.
- **Never invent a gap.** If coverage is unknown, the gap is unverified — ask for the search rather than recommending a topic that may already exist.
- **Check the framing before proposing a new field.** Most blocked directions are a problem-framing or unit-of-analysis problem, and a rename will not fix either. Widening the topic is not a repair when the real cause is that the question was never answerable.
- **"Unfamiliar to the researcher" is not "open in the field"**, and "already done" is not the same as "done this way". Both errors are checked against the actual closest work.
- **Advise re-searching before advising a change of topic.** Many blocked directions come back alive once the closest work is found; where it becomes clear they do not, say so.
- **Concreteness is the whole value.** "Search more broadly", "read more papers" and "think about your interests" are not guidance; query strings, venues, criteria and stopping rules are.
- **Respect the researcher's means.** A direction needing data, compute, instruments or approvals they cannot obtain is not viable for them, however interesting it is in the abstract.
- **Keep the register factual.** Discouragement and cheerleading both destroy the review's usefulness; report the state of the direction and the route forward in the same tone.
- **A topic proposed by the reviewer is a hypothesis, not an answer.** It carries the same obligation to search as any other candidate.

## Evidence Requirements

- The restated research question, and what would count as an answer to it.
- For a "gap already occupied" verdict: the closest works, with identifiers, and what each already establishes.
- The search audit reports what was actually searched — databases, queries, dates, screening rules, stopping point — and says which parts were not verified.
- Every recommended query string is written out in full, copy-pasteable, with the database or venue it targets and the language it should be issued in.
- For each pivot: the resources it requires (data, compute, access, skills, time) stated as constraints that were checked, not as estimates invented from nothing.
- Candidate directions carry their own verification status; none is presented as validated before its search is done.

## Expected Output

1. **Direction diagnosis** — the restated question and what would count as an answer; which of the four failure reasons hold and which do not; the discrimination tests with pass/fail and reason; verdict: **advance / pivot / stop**.
2. **Findings** — one entry per issue, with location, observation, verification status, severity, and recommendation. Findings about the **search** (coverage, missing closest work, unverifiable gap) are reported separately from findings about the **direction**.
3. **If advanceable** — the minimum steps to a plan: gap statement, questions, and the first discriminating experiment.
4. **If not advanceable** — the recovery plan: what was wrong; 2–4 pivot strategies with the reason each could work; the full re-search plan (databases, query strings, date window, screening and stopping rules); and the decision gate for choosing among the surviving candidates.
5. **Guidance** — per finding: the problem, why it matters, the root cause, what is missing, the suggested direction, the expected evidence, and the recommended skills — naming the concrete ones, such as literature search, literature screening, gap analysis, problem definition, idea generation, topic ranking and direction convergence.
6. **Next action** — the single thing to do this week, and what to bring back when it is done: the search results, the closest works, and the candidate directions they imply.

## Where the Result Goes

Write the review to `review/research-direction-review-<YYYY-MM-DD>.md` — the research root's `review/`
directory, **at the same level as `research/`**. One file per run, so two runs of the same
review can be compared and a later round can read what the previous one found. `review/`
is the reserved namespace the counterparty may write to, so anything here syncs to the
other side of an active mentorship unchanged.

Open the file with a short header: the skill id and version, the date, what was reviewed
(and whose research, when this is someone else's), and the **coverage and limits** of this
run — what was inspected and what could not be. Findings that cannot be traced back to a
source under that header are not reviewable later.

Do not modify the research state, evidence, claims, plans or paper as part of a review.
State changes are proposed, not applied.
